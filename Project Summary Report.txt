1. INTRODUCTION
On July 27, 2025, a group of students from Bogura Cantonment Public School and College (BCPSC) started
building something unique for their school: a fully functional, AI -powered humanoid robot.
The BCPSC Robot was not created to meet a class requirement or copy an existing design. The team
believed that students from a school in Bogura, Bangladesh, could create something truly impressive. The
robot could recognize faces, understand speech in both
English and Bangla, respond intelligently to
questions, move its hands and eyes smoothly, and navigate its surroundings. This belief guided every
decision made over the next 211 days.
The goal of the project was to show that robotics and artificial intelligence are not just for well
-funded
research institutions or tech companies. By combining 3D
-printed hardware, open -source AI models,
locally hosted language systems, and custom electro
nics, the team aimed to prove that meaningful
engineering is achievable with limited resources, as long as there is enough determination and technical
skill.
The vision for the BCPSC Robot had three main objectives. First, to create a robot that could act as an
intelligent, interactive representative of the school, capable of answering questions about BCPSC's staff,
facilities, and institution using a knowledge base built specifically from school data. Second, to create a
platform for genuine learning, where each team member would develop real skills in areas they had never
explored before. Third, to produce something that could stand in an exhibition, showcasin
g both the
school and what student -led engineering in Bangladesh can achieve.
Bogura Cantonment Public School and College was the only sponsor and institutional supporter of this
project. Their support made the work possible, and the robot was designed from the start to carry the
institution's name and reputation.

2. PROJECT OVERVIEW
The BCPSC Robot is a humanoid robot powered by AI. It's designed to interact with people in a natural,
smart, and responsive way. It listens to questions in both English and Bangla, understands the inquiries,
and responds with speech. It recognizes familia r faces and greets them personally. It follows faces with
its eyes, moves its hands with gestures while speaking, and moves around using a wheeled base. When
asked about Bogura Cantonment Public School and College, including details about its staff, teache rs,
governing body, contact info, or institutional aspects, it answers from a structured knowledge base made
from BCPSC's own data. For general knowledge, current news, and questions outside the school
database, it connects through a layered AI system that can access online models when necessary and
works offline when the internet is not available.
In summary, the BCPSC Robot acts as an intelligent, bilingual, interactive assistant. It can hold
conversations, recognize who it is talking to, and represent its institution.

Hardware Summary
The robot's design is a hybrid created by analyzing and combining features from two open -source
humanoid platforms: EZ -Robot and InMoov. Every structural component was custom 3D modeled,
printed, tested, adjusted until the assembly met the team's standards for strength, symmetry, and
mechanical reliability.
The computing core operates on a Raspberry Pi 5 . This unit handles all AI processing, vision tasks,
speech recognition, and system coordination. A wheeled base provides mobility. It is controlled by an
ESP32 microcontroller that manages a PWM differential drive system, features a browser-based wireless
4

joystick interface, and has a hardware deadman switch that cuts motor power if the controller
disconnects.
Facial expressions are controlled by a second ESP32 , acting as the main controller. This device drives the
eye and jaw servos directly and sends hand gesture commands over WiFi to an ESP8266 , which manages
the five finger servos independently. The entire multi -microcontroller system communicates using serial
JSON commands and HTTP REST calls over a local WiFi network created by the ESP32.
Power is supplied through a custom -designed and hand -soldered veroboard circuit. This ensures the
correct voltage reaches every component, built from scratch to guarantee safe power delivery and circuit
reliability during operation.

Software Summary
The software architecture consists of five interconnected layers, from low -level motor control firmware to
cloud -connected AI services.
At the hardware level, C++ firmware on the ESP32 and ESP8266 microcontrollers manages all servo
control, motor operation, WiFi communication, and gesture execution. This is complex, with the eye
servo implementing four behavioral states (fixating, saccadin g, scanning, glancing) and employing
Exponential Moving Average smoothing and microsaccade simulation. The jaw's movement is based on a
physics model for realistic behavior.
The vision layer operates on the Raspberry Pi, using Python to create a detection -to-actuation pipeline.
This includes face detection with YuNet ONNX, face tracking with OpenCV's CSRT tracker, pose
estimation, gaze mapping, signal smoothing, and serial communication to th e servo controller, all
happening in a continuous real -time loop.
The speech layer runs two offline speech -to-text recognizers at once. It post-processes the recognized
text using fuzzy string matching to correct any misheard Bangla names and synthesizes responses using
offline text-to-speech models for both languages.
The AI brain evolved through three generations during the project. The final production version is a
FastAPI microservice built around Gemini's function -calling API. The language model automatically
selects the right tool —whether it's for a school database lookup, new s retrieval, web search, or weather—
based on user questions. This setup includes Redis caching, Server -Sent Events streaming for quick
responses, Prometheus metrics for monitoring, and a structured admin permission gate to prevent
hardware commands from ex ecuting without explicit authorization.
The school knowledge base is a structured dataset of BCPSC records, indexed by teacher’s name, role
and blood group . The dataset is collected from http://bcpsc.edu.bd/ It uses sentence -transformer
embeddings for semantic search with queries that do not match specific keywords.

4. SYSTEM ARCHITE CTURE
The BCPSC Robot is built across five interconnected layers, spanning physical hardware at the base
and an AI-driven software brain at the top. Every layer communicates with the layers immediately
above and below it, forming a continuous pipeline from physical sensing to intelligent response. The
overall architecture was designed around three guiding principles: offline-first operation, hardware
safety, and modular separation of concerns, so that any single subsystem can be tested, repaired, or
upgraded independently.

4.1 Hardware Architecture
Microcontrollers
The robot's hardware intelligence is split among three microcontrollers. Each one has a specific role to
avoid any single controller slowing things down.
The main microcontroller is an ESP32, which acts as the master controller for the robot's expressive
systems. It manages the eye and jaw servos, processes incoming serial JSON commands from the
Raspberry Pi, and sends gesture instructions to the hand contr oller over HTTP. It works in both AP and
STA WiFi mode, meaning it serves as a wireless access point for the hand controller while also
connecting to the wider network. All commands from the Raspberry Pi are processed using ArduinoJson
and sent to the corr ect subsystem —eye, jaw, or hand—with a 100-millisecond lockout period applied
after each serial command to avoid conflicts between the web and serial interfaces.
The second microcontroller is an ESP8266, which focuses solely on hand gesture control. It runs a REST
API on port 80 and manages five individual finger servos —thumb, index, middle, ring, and little finger —
each mapped to fixed open and closed angles based on actual hardware testing. The ESP8266 has a
static IP address so the ESP32 master can find it easily.
The third microcontroller is another ESP32, which handles the drive system. It creates a WiFi access
point and provides a browser -based dual -joystick controller interface to any connected device. It
controls two DC drive motors using PWM for differential d rive. It includes a hardware deadman switch: if
no device connects to its access point for more than 500 milliseconds, the motors stop immediately and
movement is disabled. This design prevents the robot from moving on its own or rolling if the operator
loses connection.
A Raspberry Pi 5 is positioned above the microcontroller layer and serves as the main processor for
vision and AI tasks. It communicates downward to the ESP32 master using serial USB and a structured
NDJSON command protocol with acknowledgment and retry lo gic.
Power System
The hardware team designed and built the power distribution system from the ground up. They created
a custom Veroboard circuit to organize all power connections safely and make sure each component
gets the right operating voltage. The system uses a series battery setup to provide the voltage levels
needed by the drive motors, while a buck converter lowers the voltage to s afer levels for the
microcontrollers and servo motors. Strict wiring practices were followed throughout, since one wrong
7

connection in such a complex circuit could damage the entire system. The team conducted multiple
tests to confirm connection integrity before attaching powered components.
Motors and Servos
The robot uses two DC motors for movement, controlled by an H -bridge driver. This setup allows
management of both direction and speed through four PWM pins. Steering is done via differential drive —
reducing the PWM output on one side compared to the other c auses the robot to turn while still moving
forward.
The eye servo pans left and right over a calibrated range, driven by an Exponential Moving Average
smoothing algorithm that adjusts its responsiveness based on the robot's current behavior, creating
natural -looking gaze movement instead of mechanical snapp ing. The jaw servo uses a physics -based
motion model with velocity accumulation and damping, resulting in realistic mouth movement during
speech. The five hand servos operate independently, each moving to precise angles to create distinct
gestures from a l ibrary of over twenty programmed positions.
Mechanical Structure
The physical body of the BCPSC Robot is a hybrid design based on two established open -source
humanoid platforms —EZ -Robot and InMoov. It combines the best structural features of each into one
unified frame. All structural components, custom brackets, and bo dy panels were designed in 3D
modeling software and printed through several iterations, with fit testing and dimensional adjustments
made along the way.
The bottom base was designed with a focus on weight distribution, as the humanoid upper body creates
a high center of gravity that needs to be balanced by the wheeled base for stable movement. The overall
assembly underwent multiple rounds of symmetry chec ks and structural reinforcement to ensure
smooth mechanical operation across all joints and moving parts .

4.2 Software Architecture
Speech -to-Text (STT)
The speech recognition system runs two simultaneous recognizers within a single audio capture loop.
This setup allows the robot to understand input in both English and Bangla, without needing the speaker
to announce their language beforehand. English recognition is managed by Vosk, utilizing a Kaldi
acoustic model that operates entirely offline. Bangla recognition works in a parallel daemon thread
using the BanglaSpeech2Text library, also offline. During startup, the system performs ambient no ise
calibration to set appropriate energy thresholds for the microphone. A key challenge encountered
during development was audio feedback, as the robot's own TTS output was detected by the
microphone as new speech input. Careful management of audio stream timing and the relationship
between playback and capture processes resolved this issue.
STT systems often mishear Bangla proper nouns. To address this, a dedicated PostSTTProcessor
processes every recognition output before it reaches the AI brain. This processor applies three correction
strategies in sequence: Levenshtein edit distance scorin g, SequenceMatcher longest -common 8

subsequence ratio, and optional semantic embedding matching using sentence transformers. The
combined score from these methods is weighted and compared against a threshold before the corrected
text is forwarded. This step is crucial for accurate name looku ps in the BCPSC staff database.

AI Brain
The AI brain has evolved through three distinct generations during the project. The first generation relied
on a fully offline Ollama local language model with a regex -based intent recognition engine that covered
nine intent types in English and Bangla Uni code patterns. While it was functional, it lacked awareness of
school -specific information.
The second generation introduced a structured knowledge base along with a multi -source response
method. The system first searches the BCPSC database, then attempts an offline knowledge layer of
hardcoded facts, and only calls the Gemini 2.0 Flash API as a last resort. Each response includes a source
label that shows which pipeline generated it, ensuring that the system's reasoning is transparent.
The third and current generation is built as a FastAPI microservice centered on Gemini's native function calling capability. Instead of pre -classifying every query in code, the language model reads the user's
input and creates a structured function call. I t selects from registered tools, including school database
lookup, news retrieval, web search, weather, and hardware device control. The microservice executes
the selected tool, sends the result back to the model, and the model provides the final natural -language
response. Hardware control commands are queued and need separate admin confirmation before
execution, ensuring a safety measure between the AI layer and the physical robot’s movement.
Retrieval -Augmented Generation (RAG)
The RAG system gives the robot accurate, grounded knowledge about BCPSC. The knowledge base
consists of structured JSON datasets that include staff rosters, teacher lists for both the school and
college sections, governing body members, and institutional c ontact details. At startup, the system
builds two in -memory indexes from this data: a role index that maps job titles to records and a name
index that maps lowercase names to records for quick lookups. If an exact match isn’t found, the system
uses semanti c search with sentence -transformer embeddings and cosine similarity scoring. This setup
allows common queries, like finding a teacher by name or retrieving contact information, to be
answered in milliseconds without any API call. Uncommon or unclear querie s still get meaningful
responses through semantic retrieval.
Text-to-Speech (TTS)
The TTS system supports voice output in both English and Bangla. Bangla synthesis uses the Coqui TTS
library with the mobassir94/bangla -tts model. A text cleaning pipeline removes all non -Bangla Unicode
characters before synthesis to prevent garbled output from mixed-script input. Synthesized audio is
cached using a hash of the cleaned text, meaning repeated phrases, like greetings and standard
responses, load instantly from cache rather than being re -synthesized. For higher -quality output, a
quantized GGUF Bangla TTS model runs through llama.cpp, striking a good balance between voice
9

quality and file size on limited hardware. Synthesis runs in a background thread to avoid blocking the
main conversation loop while generating audio.
Vision
The vision system comprises a multi -stage pipeline designed to operate at real -time speeds on the
Raspberry Pi 5. Frames from the camera are first sent to a YuNet face detector running as an ONNX
model, which functions at about ten to fifteen frames per se cond at 320 by 240 resolution. This
resolution was chosen after testing confirmed that 640 by 480 caused unacceptable latency on ARM
hardware. Between detection frames, a CSRT tracker keeps track of the last known face position,
preventing visual output fr om flickering when the detector misses a single frame.
A PersonSelector module identifies the most relevant face in scenes with several people. It uses face size,
proximity to the center of the frame, and tracking continuity for this purpose. A PoseEstimator then
derives the subject's approximate gaze directio n from facial landmark geometry. A GazeMapper
translates this into a target servo angle. A final smoothing filter —using a critically -damped model rather
than simple exponential smoothing, which caused servo oscillation —converts the mapped angle into a
stable command sent over serial to the ESP32.
The system maintains three behavioral states: active tracking when a face is detected, idle sweep with
randomized blink timing when no face is present, and a smooth return -to-center transition between the
two. Face recognition runs separately using DeepFac e with Facenet512, creating 512 -dimensional face
embeddings that are matched against a database of known individuals for identity identification.
Backend API
The backend operates as a FastAPI microservice that provides a versioned REST and streaming API for all
robot intelligence functions. The primary chat endpoint returns responses via Server -Sent Events
streaming, with a target latency of under 200 milliseco nds for cached queries. Redis caching, along with
an in-memory fallback, stores recent responses with short time -to-live values to avoid stale answers
while significantly speeding up repeated queries. Asynchronous parallel tool execution lets multiple
Gemini function calls run at the same time using Python's asyncio framework when the model requests
more than one tool at once. Prometheus metrics are available at a dedicated endpoint, tracking request
counts, latency histograms, and tool call frequency for o bservability. The entire service is containerized
using Docker Compose with a Redis sidecar, and Kubernetes manifests are available for production
deployment. A complete test suite includes unit tests with mocked Gemini responses and integration
tests that validate end -to-end latency against defined performance targets.

6. TECHNICAL CHALLENGES FACED
Hardware Challenges
The biggest hardware challenge was merging two different open -source humanoid platforms, EZ -Robot
and InMoov, into one physical structure. These systems were not designed to work together, so
combining them required a complete redesign of joints, mounting points, and proportions instead of
just simple assembly. Keeping the structure symmetrical while ensuring durability and smooth
movement at all joints involved several rounds of 3D printing, physical testing, corrections, and
reprinting before the frame me t acceptable standards.
Software Challenges
The main software challenge was audio feedback. The robot's text -to-speech voice was being picked up
by the microphone and processed as new input, creating a loop that interrupted conversations. Solving
this issue needed careful timing and management of th e playback and capture processes. Another
related challenge was the consistent misrecognition of Bangla proper nouns by the speech -to-text
engine, which made name lookups against the BCPSC database unreliable until a dedicated correction
layer was created and integrated.
AI Challenges
The key AI challenge was stopping the language model from producing incorrect or made -up
information about BCPSC, including details about its staff and structure. Since general -purpose
language models lack specific knowledge about a school in Bogura, Bangl adesh, each school -related
response had to rely on the local knowledge base through RAG before reaching the model. Establishing
reliable confidence thresholds to determine when the RAG result was good enough and when to switch
to an online API required ext ensive tuning. Creating a system that detected intent and language equally
well in English and Bangla Unicode without misrouting queries added more complexity.
Power and Safety Issues
Designing a safe power distribution system for a robot with multiple microcontrollers, servo motors, and
DC motors, each needing different voltage and current, was a critical engineering task. A single wiring
error could damage the entire circuit. Several connection errors were found and fixed during testing
before the system was stable and safe. On the software side, a hardware deadman switch was included
in the drive system to automatically cut motor power if the wireless controller connection dropped,
preventing the robot from moving without an active operator.
Resource Limitations
The entire project operated under tight resource constraints. Hardware components, computing power,
and development time were limited, so every decision had real costs. Running computer vision tasks on
a Raspberry Pi 5 required careful choices about resolu tion—using 320 by 240 pixels instead of 640 by
480—because the higher resolution caused latency that made real -time tracking impossible on ARM
hardware. The need to maintain separate Python virtual environments due to conflicts among computer
vision, audio , and AI libraries added to the deployment complexity. Throughout the project, the team
14

consistently sought solutions that were not only functional but also lightweight and efficient enough to
run reliably on the available hardware.

7. Solutions & Improvements
7.1 How Problems Were Identified
Most problems in the BCPSC Robot project were identified through direct testing rather than theoretical
analysis. Hardware issues surfaced during physical assembly and powered testing rounds, where
incorrect wiring, poor servo calibration, or structural im balance produced immediate and observable
failures. Software issues emerged during live system runs — the audio feedback loop, for instance, was
not anticipated during design but became immediately apparent the first time TTS and STT ran
simultaneously. AI accuracy problems were discovered through test queries, where the language
model's tendency to generate plausible but incorrect information about BCPSC became clear as soon as
school -specific questions were asked. In each case, the team documented what fa iled, traced it to its
root cause, and built a targeted solution rather than applying broad workarounds.
7.2 How Problems Were Solved
The audio feedback problem was resolved by managing the timing relationship between the TTS
playback process and the STT microphone capture process, ensuring the recognizer was not actively
listening while the robot was speaking. The Bangla name misrecogni tion problem was solved by building
a dedicated PostSTTProcessor that applied three complementary string similarity algorithms
—
Levenshtein distance, SequenceMatcher ratio, and semantic embedding matching — in sequence,
correcting STT output before it rea ched any name lookup or database query. Hardware race conditions
between the serial interface and the web interface were resolved by implementing a 100 -millisecond
lockout window after every serial command, giving the hardware time to settle before accepti ng further
instructions. The eye -tracking flicker problem was solved by introducing a CSRT tracker running
between detection frames, maintaining continuity on the last known face position when the detector
momentarily failed. Power safety was addressed thr ough a purpose -built veroboard circuit with
organized, verified wiring, combined with a software deadman switch that cut motor power
automatically upon controller disconnection.
7.3 What Improved Over Time
The project showed consistent improvement in three areas. Response accuracy improved as the
knowledge base matured — early versions of the robot had no school -specific knowledge and would
fabricate answers, while later versions grounded every BCPSC -related response in verified data through
RAG. System stability improved as the serial communication protocol was hardened with ACK and retry
logic, and as audio pipeline timing was refined. Response speed improved through the introduction of
Redis caching, offli ne-first knowledge lookup, and audio caching for repeated TTS phrases, reducing
latency on predictable queries to near -instant levels.
7.4 Brain Version 1 to Version 3 Evolution
Brain Version 1 — was fully offline and deterministic. All user input passed through a regex -based intent
recognition engine that classified queries into one of nine intent types in both English and Bangla, then
15

routed each to a dedicated handler. Responses were generated by a local Ollama language model
running the Gemma 2B model on -device. This version was functional and required no internet
connection, but it had a fundamental limitation: the model had no knowl edge of BCPSC and would
produce confident but inaccurate answers to any school -specific question.
Brain Version 2 — addressed this by introducing a structured RAG knowledge base built from real BCPSC
data. A QueryClassifier detected the language and type of each query, routing it through a cascade —
first to the school database, then to an offline knowledge layer of har dcoded facts, then to Ollama for
enhancement, and finally to the Gemini API or News API when all local sources were insufficient. The
PostSTTProcessor was added at this stage to correct misheard Bangla names before they reached the
database. Each response carried a source and pipeline label so the system's reasoning path was always
transparent. The limitation of this version was that the code itself was still making all routing decisions
— the language model had no agency over which tool or data source to c onsult.
Brain Version 3 — resolved this by handing decision -making authority to the model itself through
Gemini's native function -calling API. Rather than the code pre -classifying every query, the model reads
the user's message and emits a structured function call selecting from r egistered tools — school
database lookup, weather, news, web search, and hardware device control. The microservice executes
the selected tool, returns the result, and the model produces the final response within a single streaming
cycle. This version added production infrastructure that the earlier versions lacked entirely: SSE
streaming for low -latency output, Redis caching, Prometheus observability metrics, an admin
confirmation gate for hardware commands, Docker containerization, and a full test suite. T he
progression across all three versions reflects the project's central lesson — begin with deterministic,
code-controlled logic to establish reliability, then progressively transfer agency to the model as the
infrastructure around it matures.

8.2 Teamwork Learning
Coordination
The BCPSC Robot required hardware and software to be developed simultaneously by different people,
which meant that decisions made in one domain constantly affected the other. The team learned that
coordination in this kind of project is not a one -time planning activity but a continuous process. When
Farsad modified a structural dimension, it affected where Naif could route wiring, which affected where
Jotirmoy could place sensors, which affected what Raiyan could assume about camera positioning.
Learning t o communicate changes across these dependencies — quickly and clearly — was a skill the
team developed through necessity rather than instruction.
Task Distribution
Early in the project, task boundaries were not always clearly defined, which led to occasional duplication
of effort and gaps in ownership. Over time the team naturally settled into a structure where hardware
and software responsibilities were separated, a nd within each domain, individuals took clear ownership
of specific subsystems. This organic distribution proved effective because it matched each person's
emerging strengths to the work that needed doing, rather than assigning roles based purely on initia l
preference.
Handling Leadership Gaps
There were periods during the project when senior direction was limited and team members had to take
initiative without waiting for instruction. This was particularly noted in the hardware team, where
assembly and troubleshooting work continued through coo rdination among peers rather than top -down
management. The team learned that a project of this complexity does not pause when a leader is
unavailable, and that the ability to self -organize and make reasonable decisions independently is as
important as any technical skill.
Working Under Deadline
The requirement to have a working, exhibition -ready robot by a fixed date imposed a discipline on the
project that no internal process could have replicated. The team learned to prioritize ruthlessly —
deferring improvements that were desirable but not ess ential, and concentrating effort on whatever was
most likely to fail under exhibition conditions. They learned that a robot that works reliably at eighty

9. FUTUR E IMPROVEMENTS
9.1 More Natural Movement
The current movement system is functional but operates within visible mechanical constraints. Future
development should focus on making the robot's physical behavior feel more organic and less
predictable. This includes expanding the hand gesture library w ith smoother transition animations
between positions, introducing coordinated upper -body movement so that arm, hand, and head motion
occur together in response to speech rather than independently, and refining the walking or drive
system to produce smoothe r acceleration and deceleration curves. The eye and jaw physics models,
while already implementing velocity and damping, could be further tuned with more sophisticated
behavioral patterns — longer fixation durations, more varied saccade amplitudes, and con text-aware
gaze direction that responds to where sound is coming from rather than purely where a face appears
visually.
9.2 Better Speech Quality
The current Bangla TTS output, while functional, does not yet match the naturalness of a human
speaker. On the recognition side, the STT system would benefit from a fine -tuned Bangla acoustic model
trained on local accents and school -context vocabulary, which would reduce the frequency of proper
noun misrecognitions and decrease dependence on the post -proces sing correction layer. Implementing
speaker diarization — the ability to distinguish between different speakers during a conversation —
would also allow the robot to maintain more coherent multi -person interactions during public
demonstrations.
9.3 Improved Battery System
The current power system, while safely designed, requires manual charging and does not provide the
robot or its operator with real -time information about remaining capacity. A meaningful improvement
would be the integration of a battery management system t hat monitors cell voltage, estimates
remaining runtime, and communicates charge level to the software layer so the robot can alert
operators before power becomes critically low. Moving toward a hot -swappable battery configuration —
where a depleted battery pack can be replaced without powering down the entire system — would
significantly improve the robot's suitability for extended exhibition use. Exploring higher energy -density
battery options and optimizing the power draw of the servo and motor systems wo uld further extend
operational duration between charges.
9.4 Mobile Application Control
The current control interface is browser -based and primarily intended for technical operators. A
dedicated mobile application would make the robot significantly more accessible to non -technical users
including teachers, administrative staff, and students. The application should provide real -time status
monitoring showing the robot's current operational state, active speech recognition language, and
battery level. It should allow authorized users to update the knowledge base, add new known faces to
the recognition database, and configure behavioral parameters without accessing the underlying code.
For demonstrations and exhibitions, a simplified presentation mode within the application could allow a
20

designated operator to cue specific responses or direct the robot's attention, providing greater control
over the interaction experience in front of an audience.

10. CONCLUSION
On July 27, 2025, a group of students from Bogura Cantonment Public School and College sat down with
an idea that had no guarantee of success. Two hundred and eleven days later, they had built a fully
functional AI -powered humanoid robot — one capable of r ecognizing faces, understanding speech in
two languages, answering questions about its own institution, moving its hands and eyes with natural
fluid motion, and holding a real conversation with the people standing in front of it.
The journey was not straightforward. The team rebuilt the robot's AI brain three times. They redesigned
structural components that failed under physical testing. They debugged audio pipelines at midnight,
traced wiring faults in live circuits, and rewrote software systems they had already considered finished.
Every major capability the robot demonstrates today exists because a specific problem was
encountered, understood, and solved — not because the path was clear from the beginning.
What the BCPSC Robot represents is therefore something larger than the robot itself. It is a
demonstration that advanced engineering is not the exclusive domain of universities, technology
companies, or well -funded research institutions. It is proof that s tudents from a school in Bogura,
Bangladesh, working with limited resources, shared hardware, and their own developing skills, can
produce something technically sophisticated and genuinely meaningful. The project touched embedded
systems, computer vision, natural language processing, multilingual speech, electronics, structural
design, and production software architecture — not superficially, but with the depth that comes from
building something that actually had to work.
For Bogura Cantonment Public School and College, this project carries particular significance. The robot
was built to represent the institution — to carry its name, answer questions about its people, and stand
as evidence of what its students are capable o f. It sets a precedent for the kind of work that is possible
within these walls, and establishes a foundation that future students can build upon, improve, and take
further. The knowledge, the codebase, the hardware designs, and the lessons documented in t his report
do not belong only to the team that created them. They belong to the institution, and to every student
who comes after.
The BCPSC Robot is not a finished product. It is a beginning .
